Measuring whether image generators can depict an emotion

A controlled comparison of three text-to-image generators on a narrow question — not whether the images are good, but whether they depict the emotion they were asked to depict. Includes a snapshot of a generation of language models captured shortly before they were switched off.

Role
Experiment implementation, data collection, statistical analysis
Methods
Within-subjects factorial design, crowdsourced ratings, three-way repeated-measures ANOVA with effect sizes, reward-model correlation
Stack
Python, R, JavaScript, jsPsych, OpenAI API, local inference
Output
Co-authored peer-reviewed publication; archived generation dataset

The question

Text-to-image generators are usually evaluated on image quality and on prompt fidelity in a general sense — is this a picture of a dog, is it a good picture. The question here was narrower and harder to measure: when you ask for an image depicting a specific emotion, does the result actually read as that emotion to a human being?

This matters beyond aesthetics. Emotional legibility is a functional requirement for any system meant to communicate with people — the argument for it in clinical and conversational contexts rests on empathy being tied to outcomes, which means a system that expresses the wrong affect is not merely unpolished but wrong. And it is a good test case for measurement method, because emotional depiction is genuinely subjective without being arbitrary. People do agree, imperfectly, and that imperfect agreement is the signal.

Design

A within-subjects factorial design. Every participant saw every combination of three factors:

  • Generator — three text-to-image models spanning roughly two years of development, from an early open-weights diffusion model through two generations of a commercial system.
  • Emotion — ten target emotions spanning positive and negative valence.
  • Context — the depicted subject, varied to test whether emotional legibility survives a change in what is doing the emoting.

240 images total, each condition repeated four times. Participants rated each image on how well it matched its intended emotion, recruited through a paid crowdsourcing platform with desktop-only enforcement for stimulus consistency.

Stimulus generation, and a methodological wrinkle

Adding the newest generator to an existing study created a problem I want to record because it is going to recur for anyone doing this kind of longitudinal model comparison.

The older generators accept a prompt and produce an image from that exact prompt. The newest one, at the time, was only reachable through a chat interface that rewrote the prompt before generation — expanding a short instruction into a detailed scene description of its own devising. You cannot hold the prompt fixed across conditions when one condition rewrites your prompt.

The compromise: use the original prompts as the seed instruction, edit the initial message for each new generation rather than continuing a conversation (so that no prior context leaks between conditions), and record the actual expanded prompts as an appendix. This is a genuine confound and should be stated as one. Part of what is being measured for the newest model is the quality of its prompt expansion, which is a real capability but not the same capability as the others were tested on.

A related detail: labelling. When images arrive without their generating prompt attached, recovering which emotion each was meant to depict is unreliable — I could not confidently distinguish a "hate" image from a "resentment" one on inspection, which is itself a small piece of evidence about how legible these categories are. The fix was regenerating the full set locally with filenames carrying the condition, rather than guessing.

Analysis

Three-way within-subjects ANOVA with all interaction effects, effect sizes, and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise post-hoc tests, computed in R from raw response data. I have written separately about why I re-derived this rather than accepting the analysis I was handed, and about the terminology disagreement over whether the design counted as repeated measures — it did, and that changes the error term.

Data-quality screening followed the crowdsourcing platform's own rejection criteria (three standard deviations below mean response time, rather than an intuitive threshold) plus per-participant correlation against the item mean. Four participants fell between 0.51 and 0.68 against a much higher group norm; they were retained and reported rather than excluded, because dropping participants after seeing their data is a worse problem than the noise it removes.

On presentation: I argued for box plots over bar plots, and lost the first round and won the second. The reviewer's request was for interaction plots. My position was that box plots show the full distribution — including the informative fact that a weaker generator sometimes produces excellent results, which a mean-and-error-bar plot hides completely. My collaborator's counter was that box plots are poor for comparing interactions specifically, and that with crowded data they make comparison harder. Both correct. The resolution was to supply both and let the reviewer choose, which is the right answer to most visualisation disputes and took us an embarrassingly long time to arrive at.

Correlating against a learned reward model

Separately, I scored the same image set with a published image-preference reward model and correlated it against the human ratings: Spearman 0.3027, Kendall's tau 0.2082, Pearson 0.3369.

That is a weak relationship, and the explanation is not that the model is broken — it is that general image preference and emotional depiction fidelity are different axes, routinely conflated. This is written up at length in a separate post.

One design decision downstream of that: the displayed prompt in the alignment task deliberately omitted the subject noun. If the text on screen names the subject, some of the annotator's attention goes to whether the image contains a convincing instance of it, rather than to the emotion. Removing it concentrates attention on the axis being measured.

The archive

A side thread, and the part I expect to age best.

A generation of commercial language models was scheduled for deprecation. Once switched off, their behaviour becomes unreproducible — the weights are not public, and no amount of later work recovers what they did.

I collected roughly 14,500 generations across those models before shutdown, using a factorial prompt design (emotion × context × random seed, ~2,400 unique conditions) so that the resulting archive is analysable rather than merely anecdotal. Total API cost was around two dozen dollars. I later extended the same prompt design across several open-weights model families for comparison.

Two observations from that data:

Alignment tuning suppresses negative affect. Instruction-tuning and preference-optimisation phases inject strong dispositions toward particular kinds of output, and models so tuned visibly struggle to write stories expressing negative emotions — they deflect, soften, or resolve the negative state into a redemptive ending. This is a real and measurable side effect of alignment on expressive range, and it is directly relevant to anyone using these models for affective content.

Training-data fingerprints survive into outputs. One model family produced stories set in a specific fictional city at a rate far above chance — a recognisable borrowing from a well-known animated film. Small artefacts like this are forensic evidence about training composition, and they are only visible if you generate at volume with controlled prompts.

Smaller models were also run locally to compare behaviour under the same prompt design, and the failure modes there were instructive in a different way — the smallest models get stuck in repetition loops on emotional prompts, which is a capability floor worth knowing about.

Outcome

The study contributed to a peer-reviewed publication on evaluating the alignment of AI systems with human emotions. The instrumentation is described in a companion project writeup.

What I would flag to anyone extending this: the prompt-rewriting confound is going to get worse, not better, as more generation interfaces insert their own prompt engineering between the user and the model. Longitudinal comparisons across model generations are becoming structurally harder to run cleanly, and I do not have a good answer for it beyond documenting the expansion and being explicit about what is being compared.
