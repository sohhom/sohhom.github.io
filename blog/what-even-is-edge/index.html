What even is edge

Two claims circulate about where trading edge comes from. One says the edge is the trader — discipline, psychology, mindset. The other says the edge is compensation for bearing risk that others will not. I think the first is close to a scam and the second is largely correct, which leaves a specific and uncomfortable question about what an independent researcher is actually selling.

I keep a note titled with the question in the headline. It has accumulated for over a year, mostly because I could not answer it, and the accumulated material sorts into two incompatible stories about where returns come from.

Story one: the edge is you

This is the dominant retail narrative. Your strategy is fine; your execution of it is not. You lack discipline, you let emotion in, you deviate from the plan. Fix your mindset and the returns follow.

I think this is close to a scam, and I want to be precise about why rather than just sneering.

The mechanical objection first: if you execute a system with no edge perfectly, you cannot think or feel your way into profitability. Perfect discipline applied to a coin flip returns you a coin flip minus costs. Psychology cannot manufacture positive expectancy from a process that does not have it. So mindset is at best a multiplier on an existing edge, never a source of one — and a multiplier on zero is zero.

The structural objection is more damning. The mindset framing relocates accountability from the strategy to the person, and that relocation is unfalsifiable in the direction that matters. If the strategy works, the strategy was good. If it fails, you lacked discipline. There is no outcome that counts as evidence against the strategy, which means the framework never has to produce one that works.

Then ask who benefits. The people asserting that the edge is in you are, overwhelmingly, people selling education to you. If the edge is in the strategy, they have to demonstrate a strategy — a falsifiable claim with a track record. If the edge is in your mindset, every failure is your failure and the product is never at risk.

Where the trader does matter is narrower and less flattering: the analytical skill to identify where edges exist and capture them systematically, and the resources to keep exploiting one consistently. Both are real skills. Neither is the source of the edge. They are about discovering and implementing it.

Story two: the edge is compensation for discomfort

The other account is less popular and considerably better supported. Most profitable strategies are not secret. They are documented, widely known, and continue to work. They persist because they are harvesting risk premia — you get paid for bearing risk that others will not or cannot bear.

The argument that convinced me came from an observation about how people discuss equity curves. Someone posts a track record; the responses immediately fix on the drawdowns. Forty percent drawdown, no way I'm touching that. The criticism is describing precisely the reason the strategy works. The compensation exists because most participants look at that drawdown profile and disqualify the approach. Remove the discomfort and you remove the payment.

Stated as a principle: the characteristics that repel most participants are what create the opportunity for whoever will bear them. You do not get paid for strategies that everyone finds comfortable, because everybody would already be doing them.

This reframes the search. Instead of hunting for an inefficiency that thousands of well-funded quants have somehow missed — which for an individual is mostly fantasy — you ask which discomforts you are structurally able to tolerate that other participants are not. That is a question about your own constraints: your capital, your time horizon, your redemption pressure (none, if you run your own money), your tolerance for a strategy that is flat for months.

The failure mode this diagnoses is the perpetual searcher, waiting for high returns with minimal drawdown and no psychological discomfort. That strategy does not exist. The people making money have picked their poison and learned to live with it.

Where I think both stories are incomplete

Neither account survives a cost model intact, and this is the part specific to my situation rather than borrowed from anyone's thread.

Risk premia harvesting mostly describes strategies with holding periods long enough for the premium to accrue. On Indian intraday equity, with a per-trade cost floor and a break-even move that must be cleared before a trade is even worth entering, most classical risk premia are not reachable. The premium accrues over days or weeks; intraday square-off cannot capture it.

So for the intraday case there is a third thing, which is neither mindset nor risk premium: edge as an engineering property. Not "I know something others don't" but "I have built a system that can act on a known thing more cheaply, more selectively, or more consistently than the marginal participant." Better labels. An exit that does not give back the move. A cost model exact enough that the threshold filter is correct rather than approximately correct. Position sizing that does not blow up the one time the regime shifts.

None of that is glamorous and none of it is secret. It is closer to operational competence than to insight. But it is where the difference lives when the underlying phenomenon is well known and the margin is thin.

I hold this loosely. It is the account that makes sense of my own situation, which is exactly the condition under which one should be suspicious of an account.

The uncomfortable part

If edge is mostly compensation for bearing discomfort, and partly operational competence, then the honest question for an independent researcher is: what am I able to bear, or able to build, that the marginal participant is not?

The answers available to me are narrow and worth stating plainly. I can hold a position through a drawdown that would trigger a redemption for someone managing outside money — nobody can fire me. I can spend a year on infrastructure with no returns, which no fund could justify. I can trade a universe too small to be worth an institution's attention, because a strategy that makes a few thousand rupees a day is not a business for anyone with real capital but is a perfectly good result for me.

What I cannot do is compete on latency, on data breadth, or on capital. The firms doing that in India have revenues in the thousands of crores and hardware I cannot approach. Any strategy whose edge is speed is not available.

That is a small set of advantages. But it is a specific set, which is more than the mindset story ever offers, and each item on it is checkable against a proposed strategy.

The opinion

"The edge is the trader" is not merely wrong but structurally self-serving, and the tell is that it is unfalsifiable in exactly the direction that would cost its proponents money — but the popular correction, that edge is just risk premium, quietly assumes a holding period that intraday cost structures do not permit.

The practical consequence is that "do I have an edge" is the wrong question, because it admits no test. The testable version is three questions: what discomfort am I being paid to bear, is the payment larger than my exact cost of bearing it, and would this still be true if I were not the person who thought of it. That last one is the check on the mindset story, which always answers no and never notices.
