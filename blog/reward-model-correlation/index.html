A reward model that agrees with people about a third of the time

I scored a set of AI-generated images with a published image-preference reward model and correlated it against human ratings of the same images. Spearman 0.30. The model is not broken — it is measuring something adjacent to what I was asking about, and the two get conflated routinely.

What I did

I had a set of AI-generated images and human ratings for each of them. The ratings came from crowdsourced annotators on a scale, answering a specific question: how well does this image depict the intended emotion? Not whether it was a good image. Whether it depicted the thing it was supposed to depict.

I then scored the same images with a published reward model trained on large-scale human preference data for text-to-image generation — the kind of model that is used to rank generator outputs, filter training sets, and serve as an automatic evaluation metric in place of human raters.

The correlations between the model's scores and the human ratings:

Statistic Value
Spearman's rank correlation 0.3027
Kendall's tau 0.2082
Pearson's r (raw values) 0.3369

For a model whose stated purpose is to encode human preference, against actual human judgements of the same images, that is a weak relationship. Roughly a tenth of the variance.

The obvious objection, which is correct

The model was not trained to answer my question.

Image-preference reward models are trained predominantly on judgements of overall preferability — which of these two images do you prefer — where the annotator's verdict blends prompt fidelity, composition, aesthetic appeal, anatomical plausibility and absence of artefacts into one number. My question isolated a single narrow axis: emotional depiction fidelity. There is no reason those should track each other closely, and a correlation of 0.30 is arguably evidence that they overlap somewhat, which is about what you would expect.

So this is not a bug report. The model does what it says.

The thing that is actually interesting

The conflation is not mine. It is in the literature, and it is acknowledged there, and it is then set aside.

The Human Preference Score v2 paper states it plainly: preference "may be influenced by factors such as image-text alignment and visual attractiveness. While these two factors can sometimes conflict with each other, understanding the trade-off is crucial." The same paper notes that its own prompts contain internal conflicts — a prompt asking for a "renaissance oil painting" that is also "hyper realistic" sets two criteria against each other — and observes that this "may confuse annotators in evaluating image-text alignment."

That is a striking admission when you sit with it. It means the benchmark's prompt set is not neutral with respect to what is being measured, and the resulting scalar absorbs the confusion rather than resolving it. Several other lines of work in this space — dense reward formulations, DPO applied to diffusion models, selection-based faithful generation — run into the same tension and handle it in different ways.

This is why, in the experiment I was working on, we deliberately stripped the subject noun out of the displayed prompt when asking annotators about emotional alignment. If the prompt on screen says "a person expressing amusement," some of the annotator's attention goes to whether the image contains a convincing person. Removing it pushes attention toward the emotion, which is the axis we wanted. Small interface decisions like that move the resulting numbers, which is the subject of most of the rest of my work in this area.

The opinion

A single scalar that claims to represent "human preference" is a compression of several distinct judgements that frequently conflict, and reporting it without saying which judgements went into it makes the number close to uninterpretable.

The practical consequence is specific. If you use one of these reward models to filter a training set, you are not selecting for "good images." You are selecting for a particular weighted blend of prompt fidelity and conventional attractiveness — with weights you did not choose, that were set by the annotation instructions and interface of the dataset the model was trained on, which you likely have not read. Any narrow property you care about that is not well correlated with that blend gets filtered out along with the genuine failures.

This is the same structural problem as preference aggregation across annotators, one level down. When people disagree, a reward model trained on pooled judgements averages the disagreement away; here, when a single annotator is weighing several incommensurable criteria, the scalar averages those away too. In both cases the information that got destroyed — who disagreed, on what grounds, along which axis — is often the information you actually needed.

There is a body of work arguing that disagreement should be modelled rather than eliminated. The framing I find most useful is the one that treats annotator disagreement as signal about the item rather than noise about the annotator: two images that split raters 50/50 are telling you something different from two images that split 95/5, and collapsing both to a mean discards it. The distributional-preference-learning result is the sharpest version — under hidden context, standard preference learning implicitly aggregates via Borda count, with all the pathologies that implies.

Caveats, stated plainly

This is one reward model, one image set, one narrow judgement axis, and a modest sample. It is a spot check, not a benchmark. I am not claiming the model underperforms its own stated objective — I did not test it against its own objective, and the comparison that would establish that is a different experiment.

What I am claiming is narrower and I think defensible: the correlation between a general-purpose image preference model and a specific human judgement about the same images was around 0.3, and if you are planning to substitute the former for the latter, that number is the one you should be looking at first, on your own data, before you rely on it.

The generalisable habit, which cost me an afternoon: before adopting any learned metric as a stand-in for human judgement, correlate it against actual human judgement on your own stimuli. It is a cheap check and the result is frequently sobering.
