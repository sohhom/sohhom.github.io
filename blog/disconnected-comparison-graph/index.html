The comparison graph was in ten pieces

I plotted the network of which images had been compared against which, expecting a connected blob. I got ten separate islands, one per emotion category. Every ranking method we intended to use assumes that graph is connected, and none of us had checked.

The setup

We had a pile of pairwise preference data. Human annotators had been shown two AI-generated images at a time and asked which one better matched a target emotion — the standard two-alternative forced choice, the same shape of data that reward models are trained on. Ten emotion categories, several image generators, a few hundred images, a few thousand comparisons.

The plan was to fit a Bradley–Terry model. Bradley–Terry takes pairwise outcomes and returns a latent utility score per item — one number per image, comparable across the whole set. It is the workhorse behind Elo ratings, behind LLM arena leaderboards, behind most of the preference-learning literature. It is what you use when you want a global ranking and you cannot afford to run every possible pair.

Before fitting anything I plotted the data as a graph. Nodes are images. An edge joins two images if some annotator was ever asked to compare them.

What came out

Ten disconnected clusters. One per emotion.

The reason was obvious the moment I saw it, and invisible before. The task had been "pick the image that best matches the emotion 'anger'" — so every trial drew its images from within a single emotion category. Anger images were only ever compared against other anger images. Amusement against amusement. No trial had ever placed an anger image next to an amusement image, because that question would have been incoherent to ask.

Which is fine as an experimental task. It is fatal as an input to Bradley–Terry.

The model infers a global scale from chains of comparisons: if A beats B and B beats C, C's score is pinned relative to A's even though they never met. That inference needs a path through the graph. With ten components there is no path between them, and the ten sets of scores float independently. The fitting code does not error. It returns numbers. The numbers are meaningless across clusters.

I put it to my collaborator this way: it is like comparing chess ratings against football ratings. Both are Elo. Both are real numbers. Neither tells you anything about the other, because no chess player has ever played a football match. You can compute the comparison. You cannot interpret it.

The disagreement, which was useful

My collaborator's response was reasonable and, on its own terms, correct: you don't need to play every chess player to get an Elo score. True. Bradley–Terry's central virtue is that it works from an incomplete set of comparisons. That is the whole point of it.

But sparsity and disconnection are different failures. A sparse graph is one where most pairs are missing and the remainder still form a connected web — Bradley–Terry handles that well, and there is a neat result that you need only about n−1 comparisons to chain n items into a single ranking. A disconnected graph is one where no amount of additional sampling within the current design will ever join the components, because the design forbids the joining comparisons from being asked.

The second disagreement was more interesting and took longer to resolve. My collaborator's position was that the cross-emotion comparison is not one we should want: preference is always with respect to a prompt, so asking whether an angry picture is better than a happy one is not a meaningful question about alignment. I think that is right about alignment and beside the point about our study. Our stated goal was to compare elicitation methods against each other. That comparison needs every stimulus placed on one scale, so the methods can be scored on the same axis. The within-category constraint that makes the alignment question coherent is exactly the constraint that makes the methods question unanswerable.

Both things were true at once. The design was correct for the question it was originally built for, and wrong for the question we had since started asking. It took us four months, on and off, to state that clearly.

The fix, and what it cost

The resolution was a change to the task instruction, not to the analysis. Instead of showing four images from one emotion and asking which best matched that emotion, show four images drawn from anywhere in the set, each captioned with its own intended emotion, and ask which one is the most faithful depiction of the emotion written under it.

Now a single trial can contain an anger image and an amusement image. The comparisons cross category boundaries. The graph connects. And the underlying judgement — does this image depict what it claims to depict — stays coherent, because each image is still being judged against its own target rather than against a foreign one.

The cost was that the existing data could not be retrofitted. Roughly a year of collection had produced comparisons that were internally fine and globally unusable for the ranking we wanted. We could analyse each emotion separately, which we did. We could not put them on one scale.

The thing I actually want to argue

You cannot design a preference experiment and then decide how to analyse it.

This sounds like a platitude and is violated constantly, including by me, in this project, for months. The specific failure mode is that experimental design gets treated as a psychology problem — what is a fair question to ask a human, what will they understand, what will not exhaust them — and analysis gets treated as a statistics problem to be handled once the data are in. Both framings are locally sensible. The gap between them is where the graph disconnects.

The methods that dominate preference learning right now — Bradley–Terry, Plackett–Luce, Elo and its variants — all carry structural requirements about which items must be compared with which. Those requirements are constraints on the sampling design, expressed in the language of the analysis. If the person designing the trials and the person choosing the model are different people, or are the same person at a six-month remove, the constraints do not get transmitted. Nobody is wrong at any individual step. The data are just quietly unusable at the end.

A related failure I ran into on the same data: I tried to validate my Bradley–Terry implementation by generating a synthetic set where the ground truth was known and the comparisons were perfectly transitive. The iterative fitting routine refused to converge, and I assumed for some hours that I had written a bug. I had not. Several standard implementations need some intransitivity — some genuine disagreement in the data — to have anything to solve for. Perfect consistency is a degenerate case. That is worth knowing before you trust a convergence failure to mean what you think it means.

Caveats

This is one dataset, one design, one class of models. The disconnection was visible because I plotted it; I do not know how often it occurs unnoticed in published preference datasets, and I am not in a position to claim it is common. The diagnosis was also post-hoc — I found this in data already collected, which is precisely the outcome the argument above says to avoid. I am not reporting a technique for catching this early. I am reporting what it cost to catch it late.

The one concrete thing I would carry forward: before fitting any comparison-based ranking model, plot the comparison graph and count the connected components. It takes about five lines. It would have saved us several months.
