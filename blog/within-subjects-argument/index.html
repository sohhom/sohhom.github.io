Re-deriving an ANOVA instead of arguing about it

A reviewer wanted a full three-way ANOVA table. What came back from the analysis I was handed did not reconcile with the design as I understood it. Rather than negotiate, I re-ran the whole thing in R from the raw data. The design was within-subjects, which changed the model, the post-hoc tests, and one of the reported conclusions.

The situation

A manuscript under review. The reviewer had asked, reasonably, for a complete three-way ANOVA table with interaction effects, reported to APA standards — the existing version reported main effects and p-values and little else.

The design: participants rated AI-generated images on how well each matched an intended emotion. Three factors — the image generator, the emotion, and the depicted subject context. Every participant saw every combination, and each combination appeared four times.

The analysis came back to me as a long document of statistical output. I could not reconcile it with the design. Not because the numbers looked wrong in isolation, but because I could not reconstruct which model had produced them, and therefore could not write the results section around them or defend them if the reviewer pushed.

I spent a day trying to map the output onto the hypotheses and gave up. Then I re-ran the entire analysis in R from the raw response data.

The disagreement

The substantive question was whether this was a repeated-measures design.

My position was that it plainly was: the same participants provided data at every level of every factor. That is the definition of within-subjects, and it changes the error term. Treating repeated measures as if observations were independent inflates the residual degrees of freedom, which biases the F-tests — usually toward finding effects.

My collaborator's initial position was that repeated measures referred to measurement over time, and since our conditions were randomised rather than sequential, the design was not repeated-measures. There was a genuine terminology gap here: "within-subjects" and "repeated measures" name the same thing in most statistical software and most of the psychology literature, but the phrase "repeated measures" does carry a longitudinal connotation in some fields, and if that is the sense you learned it in, our design does not look like one.

We had also, earlier in the review process, already told the reviewer we would describe it as a three-way within-subjects ANOVA. So the manuscript was committed to a description that the analysis did not implement.

Two further wrinkles surfaced once we agreed on the frame:

The four repetitions. Each condition was measured four times per participant. The options were to average them and run repeated-measures ANOVA on the means, or to model the repetitions explicitly with a mixed-effects model. I argued against averaging — collapsing four measurements to a mean discards the within-condition variance, which for a subjective judgement task is a substantial part of what you are trying to characterise. I built the linear mixed-effects model, got it running, and then made a decision I still think was right: I did not use it. I did not have enough experience with mixed models to interpret the output confidently or defend it under review. Shipping a model I could not fully explain would have been worse than shipping a simpler one I could.

Post-hoc tests. I had read that Tukey HSD does not straightforwardly apply to repeated-measures designs, because it assumes independent observations, and briefly concluded we could not do the pairwise comparisons the reviewer wanted. This was over-cautious. The resolution was pairwise t-tests with Bonferroni correction — conservative, unambiguous, defensible.

What changed

The re-derived analysis differed from what I had been given. I reported effect sizes, which had not been included and which the reviewer had implicitly asked for by requesting a full table. The interaction effects came out properly specified. And one descriptive claim in the manuscript turned out to be wrong.

The manuscript stated that all participants correlated at 0.7 or above with the mean rating per image — a check for random clicking. In the new data, four participants sat at 0.51, 0.51, 0.61 and 0.68.

We did not drop them. My collaborator's call on that was right and I want to be precise about why: the correlations were low but not at chance, the effects in the data were large and highly significant, and removing participants after seeing their data is a researcher-degrees-of-freedom problem worse than the noise it removes. The correct fix was to report the actual numbers and note that those participants may contribute noise. The original sentence was not a fabrication; it was true of an earlier dataset and had not been updated when the data were replaced. That is an ordinary and very common failure mode, and the only defence against it is re-checking every factual claim against the current data before resubmission, which is tedious and which I then did line by line.

I also checked whether the low correlations came from participants rushing. Correlation between rater-mean agreement and total time taken: 0.46. Present, moderate, not the whole story.

The opinion

When a statistical result cannot be reproduced from its description, the description is the problem — and re-deriving the result yourself is usually faster than negotiating about it.

I do not mean this as a claim about who was right. I mean it as a claim about cost. I spent roughly a day failing to reconcile the output I had been given, and about two days re-running everything from raw data in R. The second activity produced a results section I could defend line by line, effect sizes, corrected post-hocs, and the discovery of a stale factual claim. The first produced nothing but a slightly better-informed disagreement.

The general principle: for any analysis you will have to defend in public, the ability to regenerate every number from the raw data is not a nice-to-have. It is the thing that lets you find your own errors. An analysis you received as a finished artefact has exactly one failure mode available to you — trusting it — and no way to distinguish a result you disagree with from a result you have misunderstood.

The corollary I like less, but believe: this makes collaboration on statistics genuinely expensive. Two people cannot both hold the full derivation in their heads for the same paper without one of them re-deriving it, which looks like duplicated effort right up until the moment it isn't.

A note on how this reads

I have described a disagreement with a collaborator over statistical method, and I want to be clear about the frame. The disagreement was productive and civil, it was resolved on the merits, and the parts where I was wrong — the Tukey question, and my initial instinct to drop low-correlation participants — I was wrong about in exactly the same way and for the same reasons that the other positions were wrong. Terminology gaps between fields are not incompetence. They are the ordinary friction of interdisciplinary work, and the only reliable solvent is going back to the raw data together.

Caveats

One study, 24 participants, one design. The methodological points are general; the specific numbers are not, and I am not reporting the substantive findings here — those belong to the published paper, not to me. Nothing above should be read as a claim about how common stale factual claims are in manuscripts under revision, only that this one had one and that a line-by-line re-check is the thing that finds them.
