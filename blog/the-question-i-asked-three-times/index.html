The question I put on every exam

A classifier that always predicts "no" on a dataset with 15 positives out of 1000. Write its confusion matrix. Compute its accuracy. Then comment on whether accuracy is a useful metric. I set a version of this question on both quizzes and again on the final, which is either lazy exam-writing or the most deliberate thing I did all semester.

The question

Quiz 2, March 2019:

"A faulty classifier for detecting cancer from MRI scans is always predicting 'no cancer', regardless of input. A dataset of 100 scans has 15 actual cases of cancer. Write the confusion matrix for the classifier on this dataset, and calculate the accuracy and F1 score based on it. Which measure would you choose to evaluate future classifiers?"

End-semester exam, April 2019:

"A bank wants to deploy an automated system to flag fraudulent transactions. There is a validation dataset with records of 1000 transactions, out of which 15 are known to be fraud, and the rest are normal. The first classifier being tested is terrible, and always predicts 'not fraud' regardless of the input. Write the confusion matrix for this classifier for the given dataset, and calculate its accuracy. Based on the above, comment on the statement 'accuracy is not a useful metric for judging a classifier for an unbalanced dataset'."

Same question. Different domain, worse imbalance — 15 in 100 became 15 in 1000, so the useless classifier's accuracy climbs from 85% to 98.5%. A variant of the same idea appeared in a third version of the quiz too, that time with the confusion matrix partly filled in and precision, recall, accuracy and F1 all asked for explicitly.

Three appearances of one idea in a course with eleven lectures. That was not because I ran out of things to ask.

Why this one

CG 501 was a core course for MSc cognitive science students. Most of them would not become machine learning engineers. They would become researchers who use classifiers — on EEG data, on behavioural data, on survey responses — and read papers in which other people report classifier performance.

For that population there is exactly one machine learning failure mode that will actually bite them, repeatedly, in real work: a model with an impressive-sounding score that has learned nothing, on data where the thing you care about is rare. Rare-positive data is not an edge case in cognitive science, it is the normal case. Clinical populations are rare. Error trials are rare. The interesting condition is almost always the small one.

Everything else I taught them — the perceptron, backpropagation, Hopfield networks, k-means, kNN — is knowledge they can look up when they need it, and which most of them will never need. The accuracy trap is different in kind. It is not a technique to be recalled. It is a reflex that either fires when you read "98.5% accuracy" or does not, and if it does not fire, no amount of looking things up will save you, because you will not know there is anything to look up.

So I asked it three times. If a student sat all three assessments, they wrote out that confusion matrix three times, in three different domains, and computed the same embarrassing number. I would rather they left the course with one reflex installed than with seven topics half-remembered.

The design detail that mattered

The hint on the endsem version reads: "start by writing the zeros in the matrix."

That hint is doing more work than it appears to. The classifier predicts "not fraud" always, so the entire "predicted positive" row is zero — zero true positives, zero false positives. Students who tried to reason their way to precision first hit 0/0 and stalled. Students who wrote the zeros down first saw immediately that the matrix had only two non-empty cells, and that one of them contained all 15 fraud cases sitting in the false-negative box.

That is the pedagogical moment. Not the arithmetic — 985/1000 is not hard — but the visual fact that every single case you built the system to catch is in one cell, and the number reported to management is 98.5%.

I also made a deliberate choice to ask for the comment and not just the calculation, on all three versions. "Which measure would you choose to evaluate future classifiers?" and "comment on the statement". The calculation is worth very little; a student can produce 98.5% with no understanding whatsoever. The comment is the assessment. It is also much harder to grade consistently, which is relevant, since grading consistency was the item I scored joint-worst on in the course evaluation — 2.20, with two students giving it a 0 and one writing "I don't agree with the grading". Open-response questions are where that kind of complaint comes from, and I had no second marker to check myself against.

I would still ask for the comment. But I would write the rubric before the exam rather than during the marking, which is what I actually did.

The opinion

A course with eleven lectures should teach three ideas properly and let the rest be exposure — and the way you signal which three is by asking about them repeatedly, not by covering them slowly.

The standard objection is that repeating a question across assessments is teaching to the test, and that students will simply memorise the confusion matrix for the always-negative classifier. I think this objection is right about ordinary exam questions and wrong about this one, for a specific reason: memorising the answer to this question is acquiring the reflex. There is nothing else in it. A student who has cached "always-predicts-no on rare positives gives high accuracy and useless recall" has exactly the thing I wanted them to have. The cached answer and the understanding are the same object.

That property is rare, and it is what makes a question worth repeating. Most exam questions fail it — you can memorise the backpropagation derivation without being able to apply it to a new architecture. Before repeating a question, the test I would now apply is: if a student memorises the answer verbatim and nothing else, have they acquired something I wanted them to have? For almost every question I set that semester, the answer was no. For this one it was yes.

There is a cost I should state. Three assessments spent on one idea is three assessments not spent probing whether the neural network material landed. It did not land — the students said so, five of them independently, and the fundamentals item scored 2.30. I cannot rule out that more assessment pressure on the neural network block would have surfaced that earlier than the end-of-semester survey did.

The part I did not expect

Seven years later I spend my time building trading systems, and the single most important design decision in a trade-entry classifier is the one this question is about.

An intraday entry signal fires on a small minority of bars. The overwhelming majority of the time, the correct action is to do nothing. A classifier that always says "do not trade" will be right most of the time, and its accuracy will look excellent, and it is worth precisely zero. Meanwhile the metric that actually matters is precision on the rare positive class — because a false positive costs real money in transaction costs and a false negative costs only a missed opportunity — to the point where I have deliberately optimised for near-zero false positives while accepting that the model declines to trade on the large majority of genuine opportunities.

I did not connect these until I went back through the CG 501 material to write this. The exam question and the trading system are the same problem: rare positives, asymmetric costs, and a headline metric that conceals both. I taught it to twenty cognitive science students in 2019 using a cancer screening example, and I now use it every day on order flow.

Caveats

I have no data on whether the repetition worked. I did not track per-question performance across the three assessments, which would have been the obvious thing to do and would have made this post an actual finding rather than a defence of a design choice. The exam-validity item on the course survey scored 2.90 and the free text contains no comment on the exams either way, which tells me the assessments were not conspicuously bad and nothing more than that.

The claim about what cognitive science researchers need is my judgement about a population I belonged to, not a survey of one.
