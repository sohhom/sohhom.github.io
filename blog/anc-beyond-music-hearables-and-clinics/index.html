ANC is not just for music

Most consumers think of ANC as a thing that makes podcasts sound better on a flight. The literature points at a much wider surface — audiometry in non-laboratory rooms, fMRI suites, paediatric ICUs, kidney-stone procedures, even gait stability. The technology is interesting because the application list keeps growing.

Walk into an electronics store and the ANC headphone is sold as a music or commute accessory. Walk into the research literature and you find ANC being deployed in places nobody wearing a pair on the train would imagine. The thesis I wrote needed a literature review of "ANC in a human context", and that review kept turning up unexpected use cases. Some of them are clinically serious, some are speculative, and the breadth of the list is part of what makes the technology interesting beyond its main consumer story.

Hearables, briefly defined

The marketing term that bridges these worlds is hearables: a portmanteau of "headphone" and "wearable", used for devices that go in or on the ear and do something more than play audio. ANC headphones are the entry-level case. Above them sit smart earbuds with biosensors, hearing aids with smartphone integration, and increasingly, cochlear-implant-adjacent devices that share a control architecture with consumer audio gear. The most-cited market projection in this space puts hearables at 1.27 billion USD by 2025. Whatever you think of those numbers, the direction of travel is clear, and ANC is part of why these devices can do anything useful at all in a noisy environment.

Audiometry without the booth

The most procedurally interesting application is using ANC headphones to replace the soundproof booth that audiometric testing has traditionally required. Pure-tone audiometry needs a quiet room because the test is asking the patient to detect very quiet stimuli; if the ambient room is too loud the threshold measurement is corrupted upward.

Booths are expensive, bulky, and rare outside specialised clinics. ANC headphones make a credible attempt at replacing them. The early results were mixed — one study found that ANC actually degraded word recognition in hearing-impaired listeners — but the more recent generation of studies has been more positive. Bone-conduction audiometric tests, distortion-product otoacoustic emissions (used to screen newborns and patients who cannot respond verbally), and routine threshold measurements have all been done with ANC headphones standing in for the booth. The economic argument here is large: a portable headphone-based testing rig could move hearing screening out of specialist clinics and into general practice, schools, and community health programs.

Inside the fMRI suite

An MRI machine is one of the loudest environments a person ever willingly lies down inside. Gradient coils switching produce noise levels well above the threshold of discomfort, which is a problem for two distinct reasons: it makes the experience worse for the patient, and for auditory fMRI experiments it directly contaminates the stimulus the experiment is supposed to be delivering.

ANC has been deployed in this setting both for comfort and for science. Studies have reported cleaner brain-activity signals in auditory regions when participants wear ANC compared to passive protection, and behavioural measures like tone-in-noise detection thresholds also improve. The technical challenge is non-trivial — the headphone has to be MRI-safe, which rules out most magnets and ferrous components — but the perceptual and scientific payoff is substantial.

Paediatric ICUs and operating rooms

Hospitals are noisy. Intensive-care units in particular have alarms, ventilators, pumps, and human conversation that together exceed WHO-recommended levels for patient rooms. The clinical motivation for reducing this noise is mostly about staff fatigue and patient stress, both of which have measurable downstream effects on outcomes.

ANC has been studied in this setting in two flavours. The simpler experiments just measure whether wearing ANC headphones reduces self-reported noise stress in nurses and parents in a paediatric ICU; the answer is yes. The more clinical experiments ask whether patients undergoing procedures (kidney-stone shockwave lithotripsy, prostate biopsy, knee arthroplasty) experience less pain or anxiety when ANC is enabled. The results here are mixed. ANC plus music seems to help; ANC alone often does not. The cleanest interpretation is that ANC is doing useful infrastructure work — making the music or the calming verbal instructions more audible — rather than acting as a stand-alone analgesic.

Aviation, native speakers, and the cognitive cost of noise

ANC has the deepest history in military and civilian aviation. The early aviation studies were ambiguous: large drops in sound-pressure level but small improvements in actual intelligibility scores. More recent work has pulled out two patterns worth knowing. First, the ANC benefit is larger for non-native speakers of the cockpit language than for native speakers, and larger for non-pilots than for trained pilots. This is consistent with a general account in which ANC helps the listener most when their cognitive system is already doing the most work to compensate. Second, multi-sensory tasks (audio plus pictorial safety instructions) get a smaller ANC benefit because the visual channel partially compensates for the audio noise anyway.

The thread running through these results is that ANC is best understood as a cognitive-load reducer, not a sound-quality booster. The decibels matter, but they matter most when the listener is already near the edge of what they can handle.

A walking surprise

The most surprising paper I came across was about gait. Hamacher and colleagues found that walking stability — measured with standard motion-capture techniques — improves when participants wear ANC headphones in a noisy environment compared to no headphones. Their hypothesis is that ANC improves the perception of one's own footstep sounds via the bone-conduction pathway, and that those footstep sounds feed into the balance system. Whether or not that specific mechanism holds up, the result is a useful reminder that hearing is not a separate sense in any clean way. It is woven into balance, attention, memory, and motor control, and an intervention on hearing is going to have downstream effects in all of those places.

Why the breadth matters

The reason any of this is relevant for someone evaluating ANC technically: the moment you accept that the application surface includes audiometry, fMRI, hospitals, aviation safety, and balance, the criteria for "is this a good ANC headphone" stop being about how much dB it kills at 100 Hz. They become about whether the device preserves the wanted signal — the audiologist's tones, the experimenter's stimulus, the verbal instructions, the speaker's voice — well enough for the user's task. That reframing is the entire premise of the thesis I wrote, but you do not need the thesis to see it; you just need to look at the list above and notice that none of those applications care about how good your Spotify sounds on a plane.
